What does a PSU do?

Lesson 1: use case of a power supply unit

A power supply unit (PSU) converts high-voltage AC (alternating current) from the wall into low-voltage DC (direct current). Computer circuits and some mechanical parts can only run on DC.

  • A constant supply of electricity is essential: it generates, transmits and maintains the electrical signal pulses that run the electronic and mechanical parts
  • A PSU is sometimes called a switched-mode power supply (SMPS)

Unit voltages

From the wall socket down to what chips can use

  • Home and office AC is typically 100-250 V, depending on geography and electricity standards
  • DC-dependent devices need only 5-12 V
  • Step-down transformers and other electrical components do the conversion
  • Input voltages vary by country, so the regulator must be set to the right input voltage for the PSU to work correctly

How PSUs evolved

They're older than the PC

Before PCs

PSUs were used well before personal computers. Radio receivers, televisions and gaming consoles all use different PSU types to manage their power needs.

The first built-in PC PSU

Designed for the Apple II by Atari engineer Rod Holt. Before that, cases, PSUs, RF converters and RF-converter PSUs (used to connect the board to a home TV) were sold separately. Even the keyboard was sold separately.

Early computers used combinations of DC ±3.3 V, ±5 V and ±12 V. Different voltages powered different parts or attached peripherals. In modern computers:

VoltagePowers
+12 VCooling fans, hard disk drives (HDDs), serial interfaces, high-powered graphics processing units (GPUs). Also motorized equipment and electromechanical peripherals such as optical and floppy drives
+3.3 V and +5 VAlmost everything else: CPU, RAM, motherboard circuitry and internal peripherals

Power rating

How much power can it deliver?

  • Power rating is the power consumption drawn from the power main
  • All output comes from a single transformer, so a PC PSU can range from 250 W to 1000 W
  • For best performance, a PSU's rating should be 40 percent greater than the calculated system load

Where is it, and what's connected?

The physical PSU, its cables and connectors

Location
  • Near the rear of the PC chassis
  • A cable connects it to the wall socket (power main)
  • A cooling fan is usually visible next to the power cord connection
  • Some PSUs have their own on/off switch, as well as the button on the front of the chassis

Output cables

Provide power to the motherboard and other internal peripheral devices.

Connectors

  • 20+4-pin: connects the PSU to the motherboard (main power)
  • 4+4-pin: powers the graphics processing
  • Other connectors for HDDs, optical drives, other peripherals, and cooling fans if the motherboard doesn't supply them

Lesson 2: Removing a PSU

A faulty PSU is crucial to replace. 5 steps.

1

Turn off the power

Turn off the main power and disconnect the power cord. Follow the main power cord to the PC tower to locate the PSU, then disconnect the cord from the tower.

2

Remove the panel

Remove the retaining screws on the tower cover panel and pull it slightly back toward the rear of the tower. The PSU is now visible.

3

Disconnect the PSU

The output end of the PSU has several connectors leading to the motherboard and other devices (e.g. HDD, cooling fans). Carefully unplug each one.

4

Unscrew the PSU

Remove the screws that mount the PSU to the tower.

5

Remove the PSU

You can now safely lift the PSU out of the PC.

Installing a PSU

7 steps

1

Insert the PSU

Locate the PSU slot inside the tower and insert the new PSU.

2

Secure the PSU

Fasten the screws at the back of the tower tightly.

3

Connect the PSU

Connect the PSU outputs to their internal devices: the motherboard, optical drive and cooling fan.

4

Replace the panel

Put the panel back on the side of the tower.

5

Connect the power cord

Connect the power cord between the PSU and the main power source.

6

Reconnect devices and cables

Reconnect any external devices or cables.

7

Test the PSU

Turn on the main power source and press the tower's power button to make sure the PC turns on.

Lesson 3: Symptoms of PSU problems

How you know the PSU might be the culprit

  • No power
  • Unrequested restarts
  • An unresponsive system
  • Failure to detect internal devices (intermittently or permanently)
  • Overheating
  • Electrical charge on the computer case
Also a symptom: a PC cooling fan that runs constantly. (This was a true/false question in your slides: the answer is True.)

Troubleshooting checklist

Work through these in order

1

Check the AC power connection and cord

Make sure the connection from the AC input (the main) to the PSU is firm, and that the cord isn't frayed or broken.

2

Check the DC power connection

Make sure the DC connections inside the PC are firm: motherboard, HDD, optical drive, cooling fans.

3

Reseat and clean the connectors

You can use a non-corrosive cleaner such as isopropyl rubbing alcohol.

4

Check the power voltage

Use a multimeter to check for the correct voltage at various output points.

Troubleshooting with a multimeter

Check each pin has its required voltage

  1. Attach the probe to PSU ground: the multimeter's black probe goes to the PSU ground (also black)
  2. Check the orange power-good pin: ideal voltage is +3 V to +6 V
  3. Check the power at the other pins. The pins on the PSU's male connector and the motherboard's female connector may not be color coded
Required voltage per pin (up to 5 percent variation is acceptable)

Testing the power-good pin
  • The power-good pin is P8-1 on an IBM PC/AT, Baby AT and LPX (Low Profile eXtension) PSU motherboard connector, and pin 8 on an ATX (Advanced Technology eXtended) connector
  1. Connect the multimeter's black probe to the PSU connector's black wire (ground)
  2. Connect the red probe to the orange wire (power good)
  3. Turn on the power supply and read the multimeter. It should momentarily spike to +3 V or +6 V

The PSU cooling fan

Heat is the enemy

  • Cooling fans are critical: they dissipate heat generated inside the PSU
  • A fan problem may show up as a system that starts up fine but eventually malfunctions or burns out as the PSU circuit and components warm up
  • Always check fans for unexpected sounds and obstructions
  • Modern ATX PSUs use brushless DC (BLDC) fans, which reduces mechanical failures, but not electrical issues
